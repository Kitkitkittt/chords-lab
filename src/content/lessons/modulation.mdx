## Modulation moves the sense of home

A modulation is a change of key that sticks. It is not one surprising chord — it is a new tonic that your ear starts measuring everything against, usually confirmed by a cadence in the new key.

<Callout title="The test" tone="harmony">
  Ask which note now feels like rest. If the resting note moved and stayed
  moved, the music modulated. If it snapped back, you heard a borrowed chord.
</Callout>

## Closely related keys are the easy trip

Keys one step apart on the circle of fifths differ by a single accidental, so they share most of their chords. C major and G major share six of seven notes. That overlap is what makes a smooth modulation possible.

Relative major and minor pairs, like C major and A minor, share a key signature outright.

## A pivot chord belongs to both keys

The standard technique is the pivot, or common chord: pick a chord that is diatonic in the old key *and* the new one. Approach it as a member of the old key, then leave it as a member of the new key. Nothing sounds jarring because the chord itself never contradicts either key.

Moving from C major to G major, these chords are diatonic in both, so any of them can serve as the pivot:

| Chord | In C major | In G major |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C major | I | IV |
| E minor | iii | vi |
| A minor | vi | ii |
| G major | V | I |

A chord like Ab major cannot pivot here: it is in neither key.

## Confirm the new key with a cadence

After the pivot, land a cadence in the target key. Without that confirmation the passage sounds like it merely wandered.
