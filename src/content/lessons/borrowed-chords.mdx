## Two keys share a tonic

C major and C minor start on the same note. They are parallel keys. Because the tonic is shared, you can pull a chord out of C minor, drop it into C major, and still sound like you are home — just recolored.

That trade is called modal mixture, and the imported chord is a borrowed chord.

<Callout title="Why it works" tone="harmony">
  The borrowed chord keeps the same root as a chord you already expected. Only
  the quality or an inner note changes, so the progression's direction survives
  while its mood shifts.
</Callout>

## The useful ones in C major

| Borrowed chord | Spelling | Flavor |
| --- | --- | --- |
| minor iv | F minor | bittersweet, tender |
| bVII | Bb major | open, rock-flavored |
| bVI | Ab major | broad, cinematic |
| bIII | Eb major | unexpected lift |

Each comes straight from C minor. The minor iv is the clearest case: F major and F minor differ by one note, so swapping them is a single-note change with a large emotional effect.

## Borrowing is not modulating

A borrowed chord is a passing color. The key does not change; you never re-hear the tonic as somewhere else. If the music stays away long enough that a new tonic takes over, that is modulation instead.

## Hear it resolve home

The minor iv voice-leads beautifully back to I, because its Ab slides down a half step to G.
