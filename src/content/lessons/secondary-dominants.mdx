## Any chord can borrow a dominant of its own

In a key, V is the chord that pulls hardest toward I. A secondary dominant lends that same pull to some *other* chord. For one moment, the music treats a non-tonic chord as if it were the tonic. That move is called tonicizing.

The notation says exactly what it does: `V/V` is "the five of five" — the dominant of the chord that is normally V.

<Callout title="How to spell one" tone="harmony">
  Find the target. Go up a perfect fifth from it. Build a dominant seventh there.
  In C major the target V is G, a fifth above G is D, so V/V is D7.
</Callout>

## The chromatic note is the giveaway

A secondary dominant usually needs an accidental that the key signature does not have. That raised note is the leading tone of the target, and it is what makes your ear briefly accept a new home.

<AudioExample label="V/V then V in C (D7 to G)" notes={["D4", "F#4", "A4", "C5"]} mode="chord" />

D7 contains F#, which is not in C major. That F# leans up into G.

## The common ones in C major

| Numeral | Target | Spelling |
| --- | --- | --- |
| V/ii | ii (D minor) | A7 |
| V/iii | iii (E minor) | B7 |
| V/IV | IV (F major) | C7 |
| V/V | V (G major) | D7 |
| V/vi | vi (A minor) | E7 |

Notice V/IV is C7. The tonic chord itself, with a flat seventh added, becomes a dominant aiming at IV.
